How to get your rental deposit back (Canada)

Plain-language guide · reviewed for general accuracy · Canada, 2026

Canadian tenancy law is provincial, so the rules depend on where you rented. But across the country, the same basic framework applies: deposits are heavily regulated, landlords must follow strict rules about deductions, and tenants have access to low-cost dispute resolution. Here's how it generally works and what you can do.

1. Understand what kind of deposit you paid

The rules start with knowing what you actually paid. Ontario and Quebec do not allow security deposits at all — a landlord can only ask for last month's rent (which must be used for the last month, not for damage). In BC, the security deposit cannot exceed half a month's rent, and the landlord can also collect a pet damage deposit. Alberta caps the security deposit at one month's rent. Know your province's rules first — if your landlord collected more than allowed, that alone can be grounds for a claim.

2. Know your province's return deadline

Each province sets its own timeline for returning deposits after you move out. British Columbia: 15 days with an itemised statement and receipts for any deductions. Alberta: 10 days. Ontario: the last month's rent deposit must carry interest and be applied to your last month — there is no "return" step. Manitoba: if there are deductions, the landlord must provide a statement within 28 days. Missing the deadline in BC or Alberta can result in the landlord owing double the deposit.

3. What a landlord can (and can't) deduct

Across Canada, landlords may deduct for damage beyond normal wear and tear, unpaid rent, and agreed-upon utilities. They cannot deduct for ordinary aging: minor scuffs on walls, worn carpet from normal use, faded curtains, or small nail holes from hanging pictures. If a deduction looks like routine upkeep, it's worth disputing. In most provinces, the landlord must provide receipts or estimates to justify any deductions — if they don't, you have a strong case.

Tip: Take dated photos or video during your move-in and move-out inspections. In BC and Alberta, you have a right to be present for the move-out inspection. Photos are the single most effective evidence in a deposit dispute.

4. Send a written demand letter

Before filing anything formal, send your landlord a short, dated letter (email is fine if you normally communicate that way). State the deposit amount, reference your province's return deadline, note that you haven't received a return or proper deduction statement, and give a clear deadline (e.g., 7 days) to respond. Often, this alone resolves the matter — many landlords will pay rather than face a tenancy board hearing.

5. File with your provincial tenancy board

If the landlord ignores you or refuses, file a dispute with your provincial tenancy authority: the Residential Tenancy Branch (RTB) in BC, the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB) in Ontario, the Residential Tenancy Dispute Resolution Service (RTDRS) in Alberta, or the equivalent in your province. Filing fees are typically low ($50–$100) and can be waived if you qualify. The process is designed for self-representation — no lawyer needed. In many provinces, if the landlord is found to have wrongly withheld the deposit, they can be ordered to pay double or more, plus your filing fee.

Common questions

How long does a landlord have to return a deposit in Canada?
It varies by province — BC gives 15 days, Alberta gives 10 days, Ontario has no security deposit (only last month's rent). Always check your province's specific tenancy act.
Can they keep it for normal wear and tear?
No. Across Canada, normal wear and tear is the landlord's responsibility. Deductions are only allowed for damage beyond ordinary use or unpaid rent.
What if the landlord just ignores me?
File a dispute with your provincial tenancy board. The process is tenant-friendly and designed for self-representation. In many provinces, a landlord who wrongly withholds a deposit can be ordered to pay double.
