How to appeal a traffic fine in the EU

Plain-language guide · reviewed for general accuracy · European Union

Receiving a traffic fine in an EU country — whether as a resident or a visitor — can be frustrating, especially if you believe the fine was unjustified. Every EU member state has its own traffic laws and appeal procedures, but there are common principles and cross-border mechanisms you should know about. Here is a step-by-step overview.

1. Check the deadline immediately

Traffic fine appeal deadlines in the EU are generally short — often 15 to 30 days from the date you receive the notice. In Italy, you typically have 30 days (60 days for some types). In Germany, the deadline is usually two weeks from service of the fine notice. In France, the deadline can be as short as 30 days after the fine is issued. If you miss the deadline, the fine usually becomes final and enforceable, so this is the most critical first step.

2. Understand the fine notice

Read the notice carefully. In some EU countries, you may receive two documents: an initial notification (which might offer a reduced fine for early payment) and a formal penalty notice. The early-payment discount does not affect your right to appeal, but paying the reduced amount is often treated as an admission of the offence — so do not pay if you intend to appeal. Check whether the notice contains all required information: date, time, location, the specific offence, the applicable law, and how to appeal.

3. Grounds for appeal

Common valid grounds for appeal across the EU include:

  • Mistaken identity — you were not the driver or the vehicle was not yours.
  • Procedural errors — the notice was not properly served or is missing required details.
  • Inaccurate evidence — faulty speed cameras, unclear photographs, or incorrect readings.
  • Inadequate signage — the traffic sign was missing, obscured, or non-compliant.
  • Emergency or necessity — you had no reasonable alternative.
Tip: if you were caught by a speed camera, ask to see the calibration certificate. Many EU countries require speed cameras to be regularly calibrated, and an expired certificate can be grounds for cancellation of the fine.

4. Cross-border fines under the EU Directive

Under the Cross-Border Enforcement Directive (2015/413/EU), EU member states exchange vehicle registration data to pursue traffic fines across borders. This means that if you commit a traffic offence in one EU country while driving a vehicle registered in another, you can still receive and be expected to pay the fine. The directive covers eight categories of offences, including speeding, running a red light, and drink-driving. If you receive a cross-border fine, the notification must be in the language of the vehicle's registration document. You can still appeal through the procedure of the country that issued the fine — but you will need to do so remotely or through a local representative.

5. How to submit your appeal

Each country has its own procedure. Typically, you write a letter (or use an online form) stating the fine reference number, the grounds for your appeal, and any supporting evidence (photos, witness statements, calibration certificates). Send it to the address listed on the fine notice by registered post or via the official appeals portal. Many countries also allow you to request a hearing before a local traffic court or administrative authority. If the fine was issued in a language you do not speak, you may need to provide a translation — though the EU directive requires cross-border fines to be in the registered vehicle's language.

Common questions

Can I ignore a traffic fine from another EU country?
It is risky. Under the Cross-Border Enforcement Directive, fines can be pursued across borders. Ignoring the fine can lead to increased penalties, collection proceedings, or difficulties when re-entering the country.
How long do I have to appeal?
Usually 15 to 30 days from receiving the notice, depending on the country. Check the specific deadline on your fine notice and act quickly.
What evidence do I need to appeal successfully?
Photographs of the location, vehicle logbook records, dashcam footage, witness statements, or proof that you were not the driver can all support an appeal.
