Divorce and child support basics (US)

Plain-language guide · reviewed for general accuracy · United States

Divorce is never easy, but understanding the legal framework can reduce surprises and help you make practical decisions. In the US, divorce and child support are governed by state law, so the details change depending on where you live — but the basic structure is similar everywhere.

1. No-fault vs fault divorce

All 50 states now allow some form of no-fault divorce, which means you don't need to prove your spouse did anything wrong. You simply state that the marriage is irretrievably broken or that you have irreconcilable differences. Some states still recognize fault-based grounds like adultery, cruelty, or abandonment, and proving fault can affect alimony awards or property division. No-fault is typically faster and less contentious.

2. Property division: community property vs equitable distribution

States fall into two camps. In nine community-property states (including California, Texas, and Washington), most assets and debts acquired during the marriage are split 50/50. In the other 41 equitable-distribution states, the court divides property fairly — not necessarily equally — considering factors like each spouse's income, contributions as a homemaker, and the length of the marriage. Separate property (what you owned before marriage or received as a gift or inheritance) generally stays yours.

Tip: before you file, make a list of all assets and debts — bank accounts, retirement funds, real estate, credit cards, vehicles. You'll need it for the financial disclosure that every state requires.

3. Child custody and parenting plans

Courts decide custody based on the "best interests of the child." This covers physical custody (where the child lives) and legal custody (who makes major decisions about education, healthcare, and religion). Joint custody — where both parents share decision-making — is favored in most states, though physical custody arrangements vary widely. A parenting plan is a written agreement outlining custody schedules, holidays, transportation, and how disputes will be resolved. If you and your ex can agree on a plan, the court will usually approve it.

4. How child support works

Federal law requires every state to have child-support guidelines, and courts must follow them unless there's a compelling reason to deviate. The formula typically considers both parents' incomes, the number of children, the amount of time each parent spends with the children, and expenses like health insurance and childcare. The noncustodial parent generally pays support to the custodial parent. Support orders can be modified if income or circumstances change significantly. Enforcement is serious business — states can garnish wages, intercept tax refunds, suspend licenses, and even pursue criminal charges for non-payment.

5. Where to start

If you're considering divorce, start by learning your state's residency requirement (typically 3–6 months), gathering financial documents, and understanding whether you're looking at a contested or uncontested process. An uncontested divorce — where both parties agree on everything — is cheaper and faster. Many states have court self-help centers and simplified forms for couples without major disputes. Even if you plan to handle it yourself, a one-time consultation with a family law attorney can help you spot issues you might miss.

Common questions

What's the difference between no-fault and fault divorce?
No-fault means you don't need to prove wrongdoing; fault-based requires showing grounds like adultery or cruelty.
How is child support calculated?
By a state formula based on both parents' incomes, number of children, and parenting time. Courts must follow the guidelines.
Can I do my divorce without a lawyer?
Yes, if you and your spouse agree on everything. Many states offer simplified forms for uncontested divorces.
